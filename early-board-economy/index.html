Gambonanza Early Board Money Guide - How to Farm Stock Fast on a 5x5 Opening Board

How I actually make money fast on the Gambonanza opening 5x5 board. Real economy mechanics, the shop structure, token types, Max Piece on Board upgrades, and the Gambits that turn early captures into Stock.

The Opening Board Is Your Real Money Engine

The Short Version

Every Gambonanza run starts on a 5x5 board with 3 pieces, and every board growth step only happens after you beat a boss checkpoint. That means the first several games of any run are played on a small, cramped board, and that cramped board is also where your economy is most fragile. Farming Stock here is not about a secret opener. It is about understanding that you earn money for winning games, and then spending that money correctly in the shop. The Economy Guide covers the full investment picture, and the Board Size Strategy Guide explains why small boards change your build.

For a long time I treated the opening board as a tutorial, something to speed through on the way to the “real” game on bigger boards. That was backwards. The opening board is where the run is decided, because it is the only part of the run where a cheap, focused build can reliably capture every enemy piece before the Stalemate Counter fills up.

What Actually Pays You in Gambonanza

Before any farming advice makes sense, get the income model straight. There is no passive income, no interest tick, and no stock-per-minute meter ticking up while you think. Income comes from finishing games.

Income SourceHow It WorksReliability
Win a gameYou capture all enemy pieces, you get paidHigh, this is the backbone
Gold TileA piece turned gold carries money valueDepends on tile availability
Capture GambitsBerserker’s, Bug Catcher’s, Squirrel’s, Race Flag’sHigh once owned
Ending a game cleanlyFewer pieces lost to Crumble and captures means less spent refilling StockMedium, playstyle dependent

Notice what is missing. There is no “4x4 mode” that pays double, no small-board multiplier, and no way to farm an infinite board. A run is 5 stages of 5 games each, so 25 games total, and the 5th game of every stage is a boss. The opening 5x5 board is simply the first stage, before any board growth has happened. Money is made by winning those games and by picking up the capture Gambits that convert your wins into extra cash.

Why Small Boards Are Actually Good for Money

Small boards have one enormous economy advantage: games end quickly, and quick games mean you get to the next shop sooner. Your Stock total is not a rate, it is an accumulated bank, so the fastest way to build it is to win the maximum number of games per session without losing pieces.

FactorOpening 5x5 BoardLater Grown Board
Pieces you start with3Still 3, plus your grown collection
Space to hideAlmost nonePlenty, enemies spread out
Enemy pieces to captureFewMore, and more reach
Typical game lengthShortLonger
Economy roleBuild the bank earlyDefend the bank, pay for upgrades

Fewer squares means fewer places for an enemy piece to sit safely. Capturing all enemy pieces is the win condition, not checkmate and not reaching the enemy King, so a small board lets a single well-placed Knight or Queen sweep the whole enemy set. That is the real “fast farm”: win quickly, bank the payout, repeat.

Remember the Win Condition

You do not checkmate anything in Gambonanza. You win by capturing every enemy piece, and the enemy King is just another piece on the board. Stop looking for a mating net and start counting how many enemy pieces are left. On the opening board the answer is usually “not many.”

The Shop Is Where the Money Becomes Power

After each game you visit the shop. This is the loop that separates a rich run from a broke one, so know exactly what is on the shelf.

Shop SlotWhat AppearsWhy It Matters Early
1 random pieceOne piece you can addGrows your pool of deployable pieces
3 random GambitsThree rule-breaking powersThis is your build, pick with a plan
3 random tokensSee the token table belowConverts money into targeted options
RerollCosts $2Cheap enough to hunt for a specific Gambit
Boss previewBottom-left of the shopShows the next boss mechanic before you spend

That boss preview is free information and I cannot stress it enough. Before you commit a single dollar, look at the bottom-left of the shop screen, read the next boss mechanic, and ask whether your current build actually answers it. The Bosses Guide lists every mechanic so you know what you are reading.

The Three Token Types

Tokens are the flexible part of the shop. There are three kinds, and each one has three price tiers, with the pricier tiers giving you better or more choice.

Token TypeWhat It Gives YouBest Used When
Piece tokenA random pieceYou need bodies in Stock, cheap board presence
Gambit tokenPick a rarity, then choose from 3 GambitsYou are hunting a specific build piece
Tile tokenA random tile change, you pick the squareYou want to steer board geometry yourself

The tile token is the most underrated of the three. Because you choose the square, you can drop a tile exactly where your main piece fights, which is very different from a random Gambit slot. The Tile Control Guide breaks down which tiles are worth placing.

Max Piece on Board - Your Most Important Early Upgrade

The single biggest economy decision in the early game is when to spend on terrain versus when to spend on capacity. There is an upgrade for the maximum number of your pieces allowed on the board, and its cost increases every time you take it.

More pieces on the board means more captures per game, which means faster wins, which means more payouts. But every dollar you sink into that upgrade is a dollar not spent on the Gambit that would make your captures pay extra. My rule of thumb:

  1. First shop after the opening games: buy one piece or one cheap Gambit that directly helps you capture. Do not touch capacity yet.
  2. Once you notice you are routinely finishing games with pieces still sitting in Stock: that is the signal to buy a capacity upgrade.
  3. Never buy capacity on the same visit you buy a big Gambit. You will not be able to afford both, and half-financing both is the worst outcome.

Watch the Crumble Trigger

Crumble starts once one side is down to a single piece, and from that point the board loses tiles every turn, taking any piece standing on them. A lot of otherwise fine opening-board games get thrown away by playing slowly after that trigger fires. When it does, stop manoeuvring and force the final captures.

Pro tip:
The boss preview is not decoration. Read it before you reroll. I have wasted a $2 reroll more than once chasing a Gambit that the next boss would have made useless anyway.

The Real Gambits That Print Money

This is the part of the guide I had wrong for a long time. There is no magic economy Gambit that doubles your money or prints income every turn, and I spent a while chasing one that does not exist. What actually works is buying the Gambits that pay you for the capture and promotion actions you are already taking. Here are the ones that really convert early-game play into Stock.

GambitRarityCostWhat It DoesWhy It Farms
Bug Catcher’s GambitCommon$4Capturing with a Pawn pays +$2Cheapest reliable cash engine, pawns capture constantly
Race Flag’s GambitCommon$4Promotion pays +$3Early boards promote fast, this is free money
Squirrel’s GambitCommon$6Every Pawn you gain pays $1Pairs with anything that spawns pawns
Berserker’s GambitCommon$6First capture of a game pays $1Pure early-game tempo cash, pays almost every game
Valkyrie’s GambitCommon$7Queen landing turns one spare Pawn into a random pieceExtra bodies without extra shop money
Beth’s GambitRare$7Capturing the last enemy with a Queen pays extraDirectly rewards the actual win condition

Read that table again with the win condition in mind. Bug Catcher’s and Race Flag’s are $4 each and both trigger on things you are already doing: pawns capturing, and pawns promoting. On a cramped opening board where pawns promote quickly and every capture counts toward the win, these two are the highest-value early buys in the game.

Beth’s Gambit is the one I build toward. Since you win by capturing the last enemy piece, a Gambit that pays extra for finishing the job with a Queen is paying you for the win itself.

Cheap and Triggered Beats Expensive and Conditional

A $4 Common that fires every single game is worth more early than a $9 Legendary that fires twice a run. Your bank is small in the first stage, so buy the Gambits that pay on actions you cannot avoid.

Capture Tools That Save You Money

Money is not only earned, it is preserved. Losing pieces means refilling Stock, and refilling Stock means the money you just earned disappears into maintenance. These Gambits reduce that bleed.

GambitRarityCostDefensive Value
Thunder’s GambitLegendary$9Capturing with a Pawn skips the enemy turn entirely
Banana Peel’s GambitCommon$6Each turn, a 1-in-10 chance to skip the enemy turn
Rocket / Jump’s GambitRare$8Pieces can hop over holes and empty squares
Demon’s GambitRare$8When your piece is captured on a dark square, skip the enemy turn
Dungeon’s GambitCommon$6Landing a Rook grants a Rook, once per game

Thunder’s Gambit is the famous one, and it is famous for a reason: a pawn capture that ends the enemy turn is a tempo swing you feel immediately on a small board. It is expensive at $9, so it is a mid-stage purchase, not a first-shop purchase. If you want the safest possible early run instead of the richest one, the How to Play guide recommends it for exactly that reason.

My Actual Early-Game Spending Order

This is the sequence I follow now, and it holds up run after run:

  1. Games 1-2 (opening 5x5): buy nothing fancy. If Bug Catcher’s or Race Flag’s shows up for $4, take it. Otherwise take the cheapest piece that fills a hole in your board.
  2. Before the first boss: make sure you own at least one Gambit that fires on a normal, repeatable action. Check the boss preview.
  3. Games 6-10 (board has grown once): this is when capacity matters. If you are finishing games with Stock left over, buy a “Max Piece on Board” upgrade.
  4. Once you have two or three paying Gambits: switch to survivability. Thunder’s, Banana Peel’s, or Demon’s. The richer your bank gets, the more painful a loss becomes.
  5. Never spend below your safety margin. Losing a run is expensive. Being unable to buy the answer to a boss you can see coming is worse than a slightly slower bank.

Losses Are the Real Tax

Every loss is a run you have to restart, which means throwing away the whole bank and starting from 3 pieces on a fresh 5x5 board. Protecting the bank is more valuable than squeezing out one more dollar per game. The Comeback from Zero Stock guide covers what to do if it goes wrong anyway.

What Not to Do

  • Do not buy long-shot Gambits that need a board state you cannot build yet.
  • Do not reroll more than once or twice in a single shop visit. $2 adds up faster than you think.
  • Do not treat the opening board as unimportant. It sets your entire bank for the stage.
  • Do not spend everything before a boss. Read the preview first.
  • Do not ignore the tile token just because it looks fiddly. Choosing where a tile lands is real control.

When You Have Outgrown the Opening Board

By the time you have cleared a boss checkpoint, your board has grown a row, your bank is larger, and your Gambits should be doing actual work. That is when you graduate from “make money” to “spend money well.” The Economy Guide and the Mid-Run Economy Wall guide are the next reads, and the Tips page has the small habits that keep a good run from collapsing.

If you only remember one thing from this page: you get paid for winning games, so the fastest farm is the build that captures every enemy piece soonest. On a 5x5 opening board that is a build with a cheap capture Gambit, one piece that can reach the whole board, and no wasted dollars.
